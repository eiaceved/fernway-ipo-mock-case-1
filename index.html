What CMAAS does at PwC

CMAAS stands for Capital Markets and Accounting Advisory Services. The team helps companies get their accounting and reporting right during big moments: going public, buying or selling a business, raising money, or changing systems.

Put simply, CMAAS helps clients answer one question: are our numbers right, and can we report them the way investors and regulators expect, even while the business is changing?

Capital markets

Helps companies raise money

  • IPO readiness: finding the accounting, controls, and reporting gaps.
  • Helping draft the financial sections of the S-1 filing.
  • Debt and equity offerings, and answering SEC comment letters.

Accounting advisory

Solves hard accounting problems around deals and change

  • Carve-out financial statements when a company sells a division.
  • Acquisition accounting after one company buys another.
  • New or tricky standards, like revenue and lease accounting.
  • Accounting input when a company rolls out a new ERP system.
  • Financial reporting, internal controls, and fixing restatements.

What a first-year actually does

  • Research. Read the accounting rules and help write a memo on how a tricky transaction should be booked.
  • Schedules. Build the spreadsheets behind adjustments, like moving revenue into the right month.
  • Gap assessments. Compare a client's close, controls, and policies with what a public company needs, and list the gaps.
  • Drafting. Help prepare footnotes and financial sections for filings, then tie every number back to support.

The IPO readiness path, the backbone of many CMAAS projects

  1. AssessWhere are the gaps?
  2. FixPolicies, people, controls, systems.
  3. FileAudited numbers and the S-1.
  4. ReportQuarterly filings as a public company.

Most companies start 12 to 24 months before the IPO

Ten terms to drop naturally

IPO and S-1

An IPO is the first time a company sells shares to the public. The S-1 is the registration document it files with the SEC first, including audited financial statements.

SEC and PCAOB

The SEC regulates public companies. The PCAOB sets the audit standards for them, so a pre-IPO audit must meet PCAOB standards.

Emerging growth company (EGC)

A newer company with under about 1.2 billion dollars in yearly revenue. It can show two years of audited financials instead of three, and gets lighter controls rules for up to five years.

SOX 404

The Sarbanes-Oxley rule on internal controls over financial reporting. Management assesses the controls every year, and once a company is large enough, its auditor tests them too.

Material weakness

A gap in controls serious enough that a big error in the financial statements could go uncaught. It has to be disclosed to investors.

Segregation of duties

No one person should both do a task and approve it, like posting a journal entry and approving that same entry.

Revenue recognition (ASC 606)

The rule for when to book revenue: when you deliver what the customer paid for. Cut-off means booking it in the right period.

Financial close

Finalizing the books each month or quarter. Public companies need a fast, reliable close to meet filing deadlines.

Restatement

Reissuing past financial statements because they contained a material error. It's expensive and damages investor trust.

Carve-out

Separate financial statements for one part of a company that's being sold or spun off as its own business.

The case prompt

Interviewer

Our client is Fernway Pet Co., an online company that ships pet food and supplies to about 1.5 million subscribers. Last year it made about 400 million dollars in revenue and about 10 million dollars in profit, and it's growing about 30 percent a year.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What does success look like: a yes-or-no on 12 months, or a plan?
    Both. A go or no-go on the 12-month date, and a ranked fix list the board can approve at its meeting in 6 weeks.
  • What exactly did the auditors flag?
    Two material weaknesses. Subscription revenue was booked before the boxes shipped, and some staff could post and approve their own journal entries.
  • What are the key dates?
    The new ERP goes live in month 8. The S-1 filing is planned for month 9, and the IPO for month 12.
  • Who's on the finance team?
    15 people. Nobody has worked at a public company, and the controller quit 3 months ago.
  • Is there a budget for fixes?
    About 2 million dollars a year for people and fixes, on top of the 4 million dollar ERP project.
  • How many years of audited financials do we need?
    Fernway qualifies as an emerging growth company, so two years, audited to PCAOB standards.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like which pet brands Fernway sells. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Great. How would you structure your approach to this problem?

Your move

Take 2 minutes to sketch three or four buckets, then walk the interviewer through them out loud.

Strong framework

Can Fernway be IPO-ready in 12 months, and what should it fix first?
1Goal and timeline
  • Why go public, and how much money
  • Key dates: ERP, S-1 filing, IPO
  • Board's tolerance for a delay
2The numbers
  • Two years audited to PCAOB standards
  • Revenue recognition and cut-off
  • Past errors found and fixed
3People and controls
  • Public-company and SEC skills
  • Close speed vs. filing deadlines
  • Approvals, access, SOX readiness
4Systems and plan
  • ERP timing and data migration
  • Cost of fixes vs. value at risk
  • Go or no-go, 90-day plan

Say it like this

"I'd break this into four parts. First, the goal and timeline: why Fernway wants to go public, and the key dates between now and the IPO. Second, the numbers themselves: can the last two years pass a PCAOB audit, especially revenue? Third, people and controls: does the team have public-company skills, is the close fast enough, and do the key controls work? Fourth, systems and the plan: is the new ERP timed safely, what do the fixes cost compared with the risk, and is it a go or a no-go? My early guess is that controls and the ERP timing are the biggest risks, so I'd like to start with data on the close and on control testing."

Why it works

  • It answers the question that was asked: can Fernway be ready, and what comes first.
  • The buckets don't overlap and they flow: context, numbers, people and controls, then action.
  • It ends with a hypothesis about where to start, which hands the interviewer a natural next step.

If you blank, "numbers, people, process, systems" works as a backup list. On its own it doesn't answer the 12-month question, so tie it back.

Exhibit 1: the finance team under strain

Interviewer

Good place to start. The CFO shared six quarters of finance data. Take a look. What stands out to you?

Which finding should you lead with?

Revenue grew about 40 percent over six quarters, but the finance team didn't grow at all. Manual journal entries more than doubled, from 900 to 2,000. Errors found after the books closed went from 4 to 15, and the close slowed from 16 days to 25. Bank reconciliation exceptions stayed flat at 2 or 3, so the cash basics are fine. The process isn't scaling, and a public company needs to close in about 10 days to file on time.

Then

Say the insight out loud, tie it to the IPO, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"Two things stand out. Revenue grew about 40 percent, but the finance team stayed at 15 people, so the process is stretched. You can see it in the results: errors found after the close almost quadrupled, and the close slowed from 16 days to 25. A public company needs to close in about 10 days to file on time, so this matters for the IPO date. I'd want to know which controls are breaking. Do we have any control testing results?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the client, then a hypothesis-driven ask. That ask leads the interviewer to Exhibit 2.

Exhibit 2: readiness control testing

Interviewer

Good instinct. Our PwC team tested Fernway's key financial controls last month as part of a readiness review. Here are the results. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Connect the exhibits. Journal entries with no second approval and late reconciliations explain why errors keep surfacing after the close. Revenue cut-off failures mean revenue lands in the wrong month, and revenue is the number investors care about most. These are the same two material weaknesses from last year, so they're still open. Cash and inventory basics look fine.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"Revenue cut-off and journal entry approvals fail about one time in four, far above the 5 percent tolerance, and nobody reviews system access. That explains why Exhibit 1 shows errors piling up after the close. These are the same two material weaknesses the auditors flagged last year, and with the ERP going live a month before filing, I'd make fixing and retesting them a must-do before the S-1."

Risk sizing: put a dollar figure on it

This is the CMAAS version of market sizing. Same skills: state the formula, make clear assumptions, do clean math, then sanity-check.

Interviewer

The CFO wants a number. Estimate what it would cost if Fernway had to restate its financials after the IPO, and tell me whether a 2 million dollar a year fix program is worth it.

If it happens: lawsuits and settlements*
$25M
If it happens: extra audit and advisor fees
$10M
If it happens: SEC penalties
$5M
Chance of a restatement per year, today
20%
Chance per year with the program
5%
Program cost per year
$2M

*Case assumptions. A real restatement can also knock down the share price, which isn't counted here. For scale, Fernway made about $10M in profit last year (from the case prompt).

Your move

Say the formula before you calculate: expected loss per year equals the cost if it happens once, times the chance it happens in a year.

Hint: expected loss

A restatement probably won't happen in any one year. A 20 percent chance a year means it happens about once every five years. So spread the cost of one restatement over five years to get an average cost per year. That yearly average is the expected loss. Dividing by 5 is the same as multiplying by 20 percent.

  1. 1 · Cost if it happens once$25M lawsuits + $10M fees + $5M penalties$40M
  2. 2 · Average cost per year, todayAbout once every 5 years: $40M ÷ 5, same as $40M × 20%$8M
  3. 3 · Average cost per year, with the programAbout once every 20 years: $40M ÷ 20, same as $40M × 5%$2M
  4. 4 · Is the program worth it?Saves $8M − $2M = $6M a year, costs $2M a year+$4M / yr
  5. 5 · Break-evenEach 1 point off the odds saves $40M × 1% = $0.4M a year. $2M ÷ $0.4M = 5 points, so 20% → 15% covers the cost5 pts
  6. 6 · Sanity check$40M ÷ $10M profit a year = four years of profit, before any share price drop✓

Quick check: what's the expected annual loss today?

40 million dollars of impact times a 20 percent chance per year is 8 million dollars a year. 40 million is the cost of one restatement, not the expected annual loss. Everyday version: if a 40 dollar umbrella has a 1 in 5 chance of getting lost each year, you'll lose one about every five years, which averages out to 8 dollars a year.

Impact of one restatement = $40M Chance per year = 20% Expected annual loss = impact × chance = $8M a year on average
Then

Summarize the math out loud, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"One restatement would cost about 40 million dollars: 25 in lawsuits, 10 in extra fees, and 5 in penalties. At a 20 percent chance a year, that's about once every five years, so it costs Fernway about 8 million a year on average."

"The 2 million dollar program cuts the chance to 5 percent, which brings the average down to 2 million a year. So Fernway spends 2 million to save 6 million, and comes out about 4 million a year ahead."

"Sanity check: Fernway makes about 10 million a year in profit, so one restatement would wipe out four years of it, before any drop in the share price."

If the interviewer pushes back that 2 million dollars a year is a fifth of Fernway's profit: "It is, but one restatement would wipe out four years of profit. And without these fixes, the auditors may not be able to sign off in time, which delays the IPO anyway."

Score and prioritize on a heat map

Interviewer

Here's the team's risk register. Score each risk for likelihood and impact, from 1 to 5, with one reason for each. If Fernway can only tackle three things in the next 90 days, which three, and why?

Your move

Score each risk out loud with a one-line reason, then name your top three.

The team's scores, with reasons

    Say it like this

    "I'd fix three things first. Revenue cut-off, because it fails one time in four and revenue is what investors watch most. Approvals and access, because that's the other open material weakness. And the ERP timing, because a go-live one month before filing could leave us with no clean numbers to file. The slow close scores high too, but fixing staffing, approvals, and the system is what speeds it up."

    Which three come first?

    R1 and R2 score highest, and they're backed by evidence: failed tests in Exhibit 2 and the same material weaknesses as last year. R3 is high impact, and its timing is fully in Fernway's control: don't file until the new system has run clean quarter-end closes. R4, the slow close, scores high too, but it's mostly a symptom that improves as you fix approvals, the system, and staffing. R5 is part of the fix: hire. R6 is low on both.

    The recommendation

    Interviewer

    The CFO just walked in. You have 2 minutes. What's your recommendation?

    Your move

    Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

    Strong answer

    Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

    "Fernway can go public, but not safely in 12 months on the current plan. I recommend moving the target to about 15 months, and filing the S-1 only after three gaps are fixed and retested."

    "First, revenue. One in four sampled orders was booked in the wrong month. Revenue is the number investors watch most, and a restatement would cost about 40 million dollars, which is four years of profit."

    "Second, approvals and access. 30 percent of journal entries had no second approval, and nobody reviews who can get into the accounting system. These are the same two material weaknesses the auditors flagged last year."

    "Third, timing. Fixing a control takes weeks, but proving it works takes quarters. The auditors need to see the fixes work at real quarter-end closes, in the new ERP that goes live in month 8. The first two quarter-ends on the new system are months 9 and 12. So Fernway files in month 12, and with the same three months from filing to IPO as today's plan, goes public around month 15."

    "The fixes cost about 2 million dollars a year and cut the average yearly cost of a restatement from about 8 million to 2 million, so they pay for themselves three times over. The main risk is that the IPO market cools during the extra three months. To manage that, keep drafting the S-1 in parallel so Fernway can file as soon as the controls pass. Next steps: hire a controller and an SEC reporting lead within 60 days, fix revenue cut-off, rebuild approvals in the new ERP, and have us retest before filing."

    Why 15 months if the fixes take 90 days?

    Because fixing a control and proving it are different. In 90 days, Fernway can redesign the controls and pass a first retest. But auditors only treat a material weakness as fixed once the control has worked for a while, usually through more than one quarter-end close. And Fernway switches to a new ERP in month 8, so the fixes have to be proven again in the new system, at the quarter-ends in months 9 and 12. Filing before then means filing numbers nobody can vouch for yet.

    This is a common pushback. If the interviewer asks, "If the fixes take 90 days, why not keep the 12-month date?", give the answer above in two sentences.

    Market growth: capital markets and accounting advisory

    Interviewer

    Will demand for capital markets and accounting advisory keep growing?

    Your move

    Split growth into more deals and more work per deal, then list drivers and headwinds.

    Strong answer

    Market growth = more deals × more work per deal
    Deal pipeline

    Many companies have stayed private longer and are waiting to go public or be sold. Every deal needs accounting and reporting help.

    Carve-outs and M&A

    When a company sells a division, both sides need carve-out financials and acquisition accounting.

    New rules and scrutiny

    New FASB disclosure standards, like more detail on expenses and income taxes, plus steady SEC review of filings, mean more technical work.

    Systems and talent

    ERP and cloud rollouts change how accounting runs, and many finance teams are short on experienced accountants.

    Headwinds

    • IPOs and deals swing with markets. When markets fall, deals pause.
    • Private capital lets companies raise money without going public.
    • Automation and AI make routine accounting work faster, which pushes prices down.

    Quick math

    If IPO readiness work grows 12 percent a year, how long until it doubles? Use the rule of 72: 72 divided by 12 is 6 years.

    "I expect growth over time, but it will be lumpy. Deals, carve-outs, and new rules keep adding work, while a market downturn can pause IPOs for a year or two. The winners will sell judgment on hard accounting questions, not just extra hands."
